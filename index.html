Lessons

Written for this tutorial and worked through in order. Each one ends in code I actually compiled and ran on my machine; the commands and their output are real, not illustrative.

  • Lesson 1 — Four Programs, Not One The four separate programs hiding behind one clang++ command: preprocessor, compiler, assembler, linker.
  • Lesson 2 — Two Builds, One Source Tree Why one source tree needs both a debug and a release build, and how to keep the two from fighting.
  • Lesson 3 — The Linker Only Sees What You Listed Undefined symbol errors, and why dropping a .cpp into src/ does not put it in the build.
  • Lesson 4 — CMake Is Not a Build System CMake is a generator, not a build system. What it actually buys you is the dependency graph.
  • Lesson 5 — The Heap, and Your First Vector Stack versus heap, and a forty-line Vector that grows — containing a deliberate leak to find with a tool.
  • Lesson 6 — The Destructor, and the Bug It Creates One function fixes the leak from Lesson 5 — and immediately turns a silent leak into a double free. RAII, and why the destructor is never the whole answer.
  • Lesson 7 — The Copy Constructor, and Deep Copy Replacing the = delete stopgap with a real copy: a second buffer, the two signatures that will not compile, and the measured truth about the self-assignment guard.
  • Lesson 8 — Move Semantics, and the noexcept That Pays Your destructor silently deleted both move operations, so every move in the program is a copy. Writing the last two of the five, with the allocation counts measured on this machine.
  • Lesson 9 — Vector<T>, and the Bug int Was Hiding Templating the container is mostly mechanical. What it is really for is exposing a wasteful grow() that was invisible while T was int - measured, 43 allocations down to 36.
  • Lesson 10 — Iterators, and the Crash That Names Its Own Cause Six lines give your Vector begin() and end(), and std::sort starts working on it. Then the same six lines let you reproduce iterator invalidation under ASan, with grow() named in the stack trace.
  • Lesson 11 — Buckets, and the One Line That Makes It a Map A hash map is an array plus one modulo. Build HashMap on top of your own Vector with separate chaining, std::hash, and std::optional lookups - and learn why collisions are the normal case, not the error case.
  • Lesson 12 — Load Factor, and Why Rehash Is Not grow() A fixed bucket count makes the O(1) claim false: 800 keys in 8 buckets is a chain of 112. Rehashing fixes it - but it cannot reuse Vector::grow, because every element's index is recomputed rather than preserved.
  • Lesson 13 — Iterating a Bucketed Structure, and the Five Typedefs A hash map iterator holds a position in two dimensions and has to skip empty buckets. Then range-for works but std::count_if does not - and the fix is five typedefs that explain what iterator_traits is for.
  • Lesson 14 — Measuring It, and the Preset That Lies Benchmark both containers against the standard library with . The dev preset says your vector beats std::vector by 2.4x. At -O2 it does not. Then find out why insert is 5x slower, by counting copies.
  • Recall — Weeks 1–2 Interactive recall over weeks 1–2: the build pipeline, CMake, the heap, and the first Vector.

Learning records

One file per thing that cost me real time to figure out, with the evidence that settled it. These are the ones where the answer was not in any tutorial.

  • Apple clang's AddressSanitizer is unusable on this machine
  • Project 1's build is a header-only INTERFACE library with two presets
  • A .cpp in src/ is not in the build until add_executable names it
  • CMake earns its place through the dependency graph, not through convenience
  • AddressSanitizer reports no leaks on macOS; leaks fills the gap
  • -fsanitize=undefined makes Apple's leaks report zero leaks
  • A heap-corruption abort under the dev preset prints nothing at all
  • In copy assignment, the ordering is the fix; the self-assignment guard is an optimization
  • A container tested only at T = int cannot see its own unnecessary copies
  • An unoptimized benchmark does not understate the result, it reverses it
  • new T[n] is one root cause with three separate symptoms, and now it has a price
  • A median of five inside one run is not a stable result
